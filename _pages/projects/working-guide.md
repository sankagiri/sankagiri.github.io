You do not need to know how to do research before starting. Begin with something you can observe, make, or puzzle over. Work alone or with a friend. These pages offer starting points, not assignments: you can skip an extension, change the question, or discover that the interesting part is somewhere else.

## Make something small happen

Record a cooling cup. Count a queue. Read three company reports. Get one simulated room to warm up and cool down. Before building a large system, look closely at a tiny example you can understand.

Ask: **what surprised me, and what could I try next to understand it?** You do not need a grand research question on day one. If nothing works, shrink the problem until you can see where it goes wrong.

Most first steps need only an ordinary laptop, sometimes a phone. Borrow equipment where possible; read the [instrument guide]({{ '/projects/instrument-guide/' | relative_url }}) before buying. Choose more compute or better instruments when you can explain what they would let you learn.

## Use AI generously—and stay curious

Ask AI to explain unfamiliar physics, suggest experiments, write code, debug, or help you read a paper. There is no prize for doing everything unaided. But run what it writes, inspect examples, and check the claims that matter to your conclusion.

A useful request is: “Explain this idea with a tiny example I can check by hand. What assumptions does it make? How could I tell if it is wrong?” Work out the example yourself. You should be able to explain your main decisions, even if an assistant wrote much of the code. Note where AI helped and where you disagreed with it.

## A few habits that save wasted effort

- **Keep the original evidence.** Save raw readings, source links, units, dates, and a few notes or photos of the setup. Do not overwrite raw data when cleaning it.
- **Try to fool your explanation.** Repeat an experiment, swap positions, check another day, or inspect the records behind a surprising chart. Ask what else could produce the same result.
- **Compare with something simple.** A straight line, a physics equation, or an ordinary rule can be surprisingly hard to beat. A neural network is an option, not the goal.
- **Test on something new.** If you tune a model on one recording, test on another recording—not neighbouring frames from the same one. Choose new days, people, or objects according to what you want to claim.

For a simulator, a convincing animation is only the beginning. Check simple cases with known answers and compare with observations where possible. Change uncertain assumptions: does your preferred intervention still help? Results in a fictional world are conclusions about that world until you have evidence connecting it to reality.

For recordings or public data, check permission for collecting, processing, and sharing. These are different permissions; publicly readable does not always mean freely reusable.

## Let the project change

Keep an informal notebook: what you tried, what happened, and what you might try next. Failed experiments and detours belong there too. A friend who asks “how do you know?” can be very useful.

You can stop when you have something worth explaining, even if it differs from the original plan. Share the question, evidence, code or setup, and the biggest limitation. A modest observation with a careful explanation is valuable.
